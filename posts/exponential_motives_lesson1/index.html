These are notes from a lecture by J. Fresán on Exponential Motives, a generalisation of the theory of motives. There are many ways to think about motives; the perspective here is close to Nori motives, since periods are central.

I. Periods

Let $k \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$ be a subfield of the complex numbers. The most interesting cases are $k = \mathbb{Q}$ or $k = \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.

Let $X$ be a smooth algebraic variety over $k$. Most of the time we assume $X$ is affine. We attach to $X$ two vector spaces over $k$:

a) Algebraic de Rham Cohomology

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$$

This is a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space.

b) Betti Cohomology

$$H^*_B(X) = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} H^n_B(X)$$

This is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space. (This uses the fact that $k \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$; the de Rham side does not.)

c) Comparison Isomorphism

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \otimes_k \mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_B(X) \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{C}$$

Algebraic de Rham Cohomology

On $X$ we have $\Omega^1_{X/k}$, the sheaf of Kähler differentials. Think of it as the algebraic analogue of differential forms on a smooth manifold: locally you have generators $dx_i$, and you can define 1-forms $f_i\, dx_i$ where the $f_i$ are now algebraic (rather than $C^\infty$) functions.

Fact. If $X$ is smooth, then $\Omega^1_{X/k}$ is locally free (hence coherent).

We form the algebraic de Rham complex (a complex of coherent sheaves in the Zariski topology):

$$\mathcal{O}_X \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^1_{X/k} \longrightarrow \Omega^2_{X/k} \longrightarrow \cdots \qquad \text{where } \Omega^p_{X/k} = \wedge^p \Omega^1_{X/k}.$$

Denote this complex $\Omega^\bullet_{X/k}$.

Definition.

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) := \mathbb{H}^*_{\mathrm{Zar}}(X,\, \Omega^\bullet_{X/k})$$

(hypercohomology of that complex of sheaves).

Remark. There is a spectral sequence

$$E_1^{p,q} = H^q(X,\, \Omega^p_X) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X).$$

Remark (affine case). If $X$ is affine, coherent sheaves have no positive cohomology, so $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X) = 0$ for all $q \geq 1$. The spectral sequence degenerates at the first page, and therefore:

$$H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) = H^n\!\left(\mathcal{O}_X(X) \to \Omega^1_X(X) \to \Omega^2_X(X) \to \cdots\right)$$

i.e. the cohomology of the complex of global sections. This is finite-dimensional. In particular, an element of $H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$ is the class $[\omega]$ of some $\omega \in \Omega^n_X(X)$ with $d\omega = 0$ (closed).

Remark. If $X$ is not affine, it is not easy to write explicit examples of forms. One usually passes through hypercohomology. For instance, if $X = \bigcup_i U_i$ is a union of affines, an element of $H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$ looks like a tuple

$$\left([\omega_{U_i}]_{U_i},\ [\eta_{U_i \cap U_j}]_{U_i \cap U_j},\ \ldots\right).$$

Exercise. Try this for $\mathbb{P}^1$, which has 2 affine opens.

Betti Cohomology (= Singular Cohomology)

("Betti" is standard terminology in algebraic geometry.)

Since $k \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$, we can consider

$$X(\mathbb{C}) = \mathrm{Hom}_k(\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{C},\, X).$$

This is a nice topological space — in fact a complex manifold (because $X$ is smooth). So we can take singular cohomology:

$$H^*_B(X) := H^*_{\mathrm{sing}}(X(\mathbb{C}),\, \mathbb{Q}) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}}\!\left(H^B_*(X),\, \mathbb{Q}\right).$$

We work with $\mathbb{Q}$-coefficients: by the universal coefficient theorem, $H^*_{\mathrm{sing}}(X(\mathbb{C}), \mathbb{Q})$ is exactly the $\mathbb{Q}$-dual of the singular homology $H^B_*(X)$.

Recall singular homology:

$$C_p(X(\mathbb{C})) = \bigoplus_{\sigma:\Delta^p \to X(\mathbb{C})} \mathbb{Q} \cdot \sigma, \qquad \Delta^p = \{(x_0,\ldots,x_p)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1} : x_i \geq 0,\ \textstyle\sum x_i = 1\}.$$

The boundary maps are given by the alternating sum of face inclusions $\partial_i: \Delta^{p-1} \to \Delta^p$. An element of $H^B_n(X(\mathbb{C}))$ is a class $[\sigma]$ of some $\sigma: \Delta^n \to X(\mathbb{C})$ with $\partial\sigma = 0$.

Proposition.

  1. The homology does not change if we restrict to $C^\infty$-simplices, i.e. there exists an open neighbourhood $U$ of $\Delta^n$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ such that any $\sigma: \Delta^n \to X(\mathbb{C})$ extends to a $C^\infty$-function $\tilde\sigma: U \to X(\mathbb{C})$.
  2. $H^*_B(X)$ agrees with sheaf cohomology: $H^*(X(\mathbb{C}),\, \underline{\mathbb{Q}}_{X(\mathbb{C})})$, where $\underline{\mathbb{Q}}_{X(\mathbb{C})}$ is the constant sheaf of locally constant functions.

The De Rham–Grothendieck Theorem

Theorem (De Rham, Grothendieck). There is a canonical isomorphism

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \otimes_k \mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_B(X) \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{C},$$

which is dual to a perfect pairing:

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \otimes H^B_*(X) \longrightarrow \mathbb{C}.$$

This pairing is uniquely determined by what it does on affine varieties. On an affine $X$, it is given by:

$$[\omega] \otimes [\sigma] \longmapsto \int_\sigma \omega,$$

where for $\sigma = \sum n_i \sigma_i$ with $\sigma_i: \Delta^n \to X(\mathbb{C})$, we set $\int_\sigma \omega = \sum n_i \int_{\Delta^n} \sigma_i^*(\omega)$.

Remark. The pairing is well-defined and independent of the choice of representatives: $\int_\sigma d\eta = \int_{\partial\sigma} \eta = 0$, and similarly changing $\omega$ by an exact form gives zero.

Remark. Note that tensoring with $k$ is not enough — one needs $\mathbb{C}$! Indeed, $H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$ and $H^*_B(X)$ are isomorphic over $\mathbb{Q}$ (as abstract vector spaces of the same dimension), but the only way to write down the isomorphism is over $\mathbb{C}$.

The isomorphism is canonical, meaning it is functorial: given a morphism $f: X \to Y$ of algebraic varieties over $k$, the diagram

$$\begin{array}{ccc} H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \otimes H^B_*(X) & \longrightarrow & \mathbb{C} \\ \uparrow f^* \quad \downarrow f_* & & \| \\ H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(Y) \otimes H^B_*(Y) & \longrightarrow & \mathbb{C} \end{array}$$

commutes. This boils down to the change-of-variables formula $\int_\sigma f^*\omega = \int_{f_*\sigma} \omega$.

Periods: Preliminary Definition

Preliminary Definition. A period is a complex number that can be written as $\int_\sigma \omega$ for some $\omega \in H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$ and $\sigma \in H^B_n(X)$, for some smooth algebraic variety $X$ defined over $k \subset \overline{\mathbb{Q}} \subset \mathbb{C}$.

In other words, periods are exactly the entries of the comparison isomorphism matrix (once bases are chosen).

Warning. The isomorphism requires fixing bases; the numbers depend on this choice.

Why "preliminary"? All such numbers will be periods, but in reality we want more. For example:

$$\log 2 = \int_1^2 \frac{dx}{x}$$

is not obviously in the above definition (the path from $1$ to $2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ does not obviously fit the framework for a smooth projective variety over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$). The definition will be extended later.

Examples

Example 1: Spec of a number field

Take $k = \mathbb{Q}$, $X = \mathrm{Spec}\, K$ (affine of dimension 0) where $K$ is a number field. Say $X \subset \mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{Q}$ is defined by $\{P(x) = 0\}$ for an irreducible polynomial $P$, so that $\mathcal{O}(X) = \mathbb{Q}[x]/(P) = K$.

  • De Rham side: The de Rham complex is just $\mathcal{O}(X) = K$ (the derivative is always zero), so $H^0_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) = K$ as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space.

  • Betti side: $X(\mathbb{C}) = \mathrm{Hom}_\mathbb{Q}(K, \mathbb{C})$ = the set of complex embeddings of $K$, so $H^0_B(X) = \mathbb{Q}^{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}$.

  • Integration is just evaluation: the period matrix, for a basis $x_1, \ldots, x_d$ of $K$ and embeddings $\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_d$, is the $d \times d$ matrix $(\sigma_i(x_j))$.

For the basis $1, \alpha, \ldots, \alpha^{d-1}$ (where $\alpha$ is a root of $P$), this becomes a Vandermonde matrix:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \sigma_1(\alpha) & \cdots & \sigma_1(\alpha)^d \\ 1 & \sigma_2(\alpha) & & \\ 1 & \sigma_d(\alpha) & & \end{pmatrix}$$

with non-zero determinant. So the isomorphism here is very concrete!

Remark. The determinant of this matrix is a square root of the discriminant of $K$.

Remark. All the entries of this matrix are algebraic numbers! The periods here are algebraic.

Example 2: $\mathbb{G}_m = \mathbb{A}^1 \setminus \{0\}$

Take $X = \mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\} \subset \mathbb{A}^2_\mathbb{Q}$, defined by $\{xy = 1\}$. This is $\mathbb{G}_m = \mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}])$.

  • De Rham complex:

    $$\mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}] \xrightarrow{d} \mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}]\,dx \longrightarrow 0, \qquad P \mapsto P'\,dx.$$

    Since $d$ is linear, it suffices to study the monomials $x^n$: one checks that $x^n \in \mathrm{Im}(d)$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ except $n = -1$ (because the logarithm is not algebraic). Thus:

    $$H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \cong \mathbb{Q} \cdot \left[\tfrac{dx}{x}\right].$$

  • Betti side: $X(\mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^*$, which has the homotopy type of $S^1$. The first homology is generated by a loop $\sigma$ around the origin:

    $$H^B_1(X) = \mathbb{Q} \cdot [\sigma].$$

  • Period matrix (a $1 \times 1$ matrix):

    $$\int_{|x|=1} \frac{dx}{x} = 2\pi i.$$

The entry of the comparison matrix is transcendental — it lies in $\mathbb{C}$ but is not even algebraic!

Take-away. For $k = \mathbb{Q}$, the spaces $H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$ and $H^*_B(X)$ are isomorphic as abstract $\mathbb{Q}$-vector spaces, but the only way to write down the isomorphism is over $\mathbb{C}$.

Sketch of Proof of the Comparison Isomorphism

Step 1. $X$ is over $k$. Set $X_\mathbb{C} = X \times_k \mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C})$ (same equations, but coefficients in $\mathbb{C}$). This is a complex algebraic variety, and

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_\mathbb{C}) = H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \otimes_k \mathbb{C},$$

so we may assume $k = \mathbb{C}$.

Step 2. Since polynomials are analytic functions, we can form the analytification:

$$X^{\mathrm{an}} = \text{analytic variety associated to } X, \quad \text{underlying set } X(\mathbb{C}).$$

Define the analytic de Rham cohomology:

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR, an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}}) = \mathbb{H}^*_{\mathrm{an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}},\, \Omega^\bullet_{X^{\mathrm{an}}}).$$

Theorem. $H^*_{\mathrm{dR, an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}})$ is canonically isomorphic to $H^*_B(X) \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{C} = H^*(X^{\mathrm{an}}, \underline{\mathbb{C}}_{X^{\mathrm{an}}})$.

This follows from the Poincaré Lemma: the map of sheaves

$$\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{X^{\mathrm{an}}} \longrightarrow \left(\mathcal{O}_{X^{\mathrm{an}}} \to \Omega^1_{X^{\mathrm{an}}} \to \cdots\right)$$

is a quasi-isomorphism (closed implies locally exact — "chiusa su una palla è esatta").

Step 3. It remains to prove that the natural map

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_{\mathrm{dR, an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}})$$

(algebraic → analytic) is an isomorphism. This is a theorem of Grothendieck.

⚠️ Warning. This step will not be true for exponential motives!

Even for $X = \mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ this is surprising: algebraically one has $\mathbb{C}[x, x^{-1}] \to \mathbb{C}[x, x^{-1}]\,dx$, while analytically there are many more holomorphic functions $\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}^*) \to \mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}^*)\,dz$. And yet the cohomologies agree!

How to prove Step 3. If $X$ is projective (hence proper), one compares via the spectral sequences

$$E_1^{p,q} = H^q_{\mathrm{Zar}}(X, \Omega^p_X) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$$

$$E_1^{p,q} = H^q_{\mathrm{an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}}, \Omega^p_{X^{\mathrm{an}}}) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}_{\mathrm{dR, an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}})$$

and applies the GAGA theorem (Serre) to identify the $E_1$ pages:

$$H^q_{\mathrm{Zar}}(X, \Omega^p_X) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^q_{\mathrm{an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}}, \Omega^p_{X^{\mathrm{an}}}).$$

If $X$ is not proper, one compactifies. By Hironaka's resolution of singularities, one finds a smooth proper compactification:

$$X \overset{j}{\hookrightarrow} \overline{X}, \qquad D = \overline{X} \setminus X \text{ a simple normal crossing divisor (s.n.c.)}.$$

Recall: $D = \bigcup D_i$ is s.n.c. if the irreducible components $D_i$ are smooth divisors intersecting transversally, "like coordinate hyperplanes." The compactification is used as follows: one works with the logarithmic de Rham complex

$$\Omega^\bullet_{\overline{X}}(\log D) \subset j_* \Omega^\bullet_X$$

and its analytification, and then proves that the two natural inclusions

$$\Omega^\bullet_{\overline{X}}(\log D) \hookrightarrow j_* \Omega^\bullet_X \qquad \text{(algebraic)}$$

$$\Omega^\bullet_{\overline{X}^{\mathrm{an}}}(\log D^{\mathrm{an}}) \hookrightarrow j_* \Omega^\bullet_{X^{\mathrm{an}}} \qquad \text{(analytic)}$$

are quasi-isomorphisms. One cannot apply GAGA directly, because $j_*\Omega^\bullet_{X^{\mathrm{an}}}$ is not the analytification of $j_*\Omega^\bullet_X$, but the logarithmic subcomplex is coherent, and GAGA applies to it. $\square$

Motivation: Exponential Periods

There are numbers we would like to study that the classical theory of periods cannot capture:

  • $e^\alpha$ for $\alpha \in \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ algebraic,
  • $\sqrt{\pi} = \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)$,
  • more generally $\Gamma(s)$ for $s \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \mathbb{Z}$,
  • Euler's constant $\gamma$,
  • $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{(n!)^2}$.

None of these is expected to be a period! Can we find a framework to understand why?

This is the motivation for exponential motives: a generalisation of the theory that enlarges the class of "periods" to accommodate these numbers, by twisting the de Rham complex with an exponential factor.
