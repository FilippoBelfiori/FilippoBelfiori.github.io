Recap: Nori Motives

Recall the category of Nori motives $M(k) = \langle Q(k), \rho_B \rangle$, where $Q(k)$ is the quiver of algebraic varieties over $k$ with vertices $[X, Y, n, i]$ and arrows of types (a), (b), (c) inspired by the Kontsevich–Zagier conjecture. The Betti representation is:

$$\rho_B: Q(k) \longrightarrow \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}, \qquad [X, Y, n, i] \longmapsto H^n_B(X, Y)(i).$$

Objects of $M(k)$ are finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector spaces with a continuous action of $\mathrm{End}(\rho_B)$.

I. Tensor Product on $M(k)$ and the Tannakian Structure

All of the above was formal. The non-formal, genuinely hard part of the construction is:

Theorem (Nori). The category $M(k)$ is equipped with a tensor product $\otimes$, making it into a neutral Tannakian category over $\mathbb{Q}$, with fiber functor the Betti realization:

$$\omega_B: M(k) \longrightarrow \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}, \qquad \omega_B([X, Y, n, i]) = H^n_B(X, Y)(i).$$

The theorem says in particular that $\otimes$ in $M(k)$ is compatible with the Künneth formula via $\omega_B$.

Why is this non-trivial? We need to define $[X, Y, n, i] \otimes [X', Y', n', i']$ compatibly with $\rho_B$. The natural candidate is $[X \times X',\, (Y \times X') \cup (X \times Y'),\, n+n',\, i+i']$. The problem is that Künneth gives:

$$H^{n+n'}_B\!\left(X \times X',\, (Y \times X') \cup (X \times Y')\right) \simeq \bigoplus_{\substack{a+b=n+n'}} H^a_B(X, Y) \otimes H^b_B(X', Y'),$$

and there are many pairs $(a,b)$ with $a+b = n+n'$, not just $(n,n')$. Even without Tate twists the naive definition does not work.

Nori solved this using his basic lemma: the idea is to ensure only the pair $(a,b) = (n,n')$ contributes, by an appropriate connectivity/vanishing argument.

Tannakian Structure and the Motivic Galois Group

Being a neutral Tannakian category over $\mathbb{Q}$ means there exists an affine group scheme, the motivic Galois group $G_{\mathrm{mot}}(k)$, such that:

$$M(k) \simeq \mathrm{Rep}(G_{\mathrm{mot}}(k)).$$

This group is enormous — not finite-dimensional at all. From this point of view, a motive is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space with an action of $G_{\mathrm{mot}}(k)$ factoring through a finite-dimensional algebraic quotient.

Remark. The Tate twists are needed precisely to ensure that $G_{\mathrm{mot}}(k)$ is a group: one needs invertible objects under $\otimes$, and $\mathbb{Q}(1) = H^1_B(\mathbb{G}_m)$ with its inverse $\mathbb{Q}(-1)$ provide these. A miracle: adding this single inverse gives all the inverses needed.

II. The Period Torsor and the Kontsevich–Zagier Conjecture

The De Rham Realization

Periods come from the comparison isomorphism, but $M(k)$ so far only uses $\omega_B$. To access periods, we need a second functor.

Using the universal property of Nori's construction, one builds a de Rham realization functor:

$$\omega_{\mathrm{dR}}: M(k) \longrightarrow \mathrm{Vect}_k,$$

such that when $X$ is smooth and $Y$ is s.n.c.:

$$\omega_{\mathrm{dR}}([X, Y, n, i]) = H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y)(i).$$

The two realization functors fit in the following diagram:

The Period Torsor $\mathcal{P}$

Comparing the two realization functors, one defines the period torsor:

$$\mathcal{P} = \underline{\mathrm{Isom}}^{\otimes}(\omega_{\mathrm{dR}},\, \omega_B \otimes_\mathbb{Q} k),$$

the affine $k$-scheme parametrizing tensor-compatible isomorphisms between the two realizations. One proves this functor of points is representable.

This may look abstract, but we know a concrete complex point of $\mathcal{P}$: the Grothendieck comparison isomorphism

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y) \otimes_k \mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_B(X, Y) \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{C}$$

gives an element $\mathrm{comp} \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C})$. So $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C}) \neq \emptyset$, which implies $\mathcal{P} \neq \emptyset$ as a scheme.

Periods as Functions on $\mathcal{P}$

Regular functions on $\mathcal{P}$ can be evaluated at $\mathrm{comp}$:

$$\mathcal{O}\!\left(\underline{\mathrm{Isom}}^{\otimes}(\omega_{\mathrm{dR}},\, \omega_B \otimes k)\right) \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{ev}_{\mathrm{comp}}\;} \mathbb{C}.$$
Conjecture (Kontsevich–Zagier). This evaluation map is injective.

Remark. The image is exactly the ring of periods, by definition — the three relations (bilinearity, change of variables, Stokes) used to define equality of periods are precisely the relations used to define the morphisms in $Q(k)$, built into the structure of $M(k)$.

Explicit Description

Given $[\omega] \in H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y)$ and $[\sigma] \in H^B_n(X, Y)$, these define an object $H^n(X, Y) \in M(k)$ and a regular function on $\mathcal{P}$:

$$\underline{\mathrm{Isom}}^{\otimes}(\omega_{\mathrm{dR}},\, \omega_B \otimes k) \longrightarrow \mathbb{A}^1_k, \qquad \varphi \longmapsto \langle \varphi_{H^n(X,Y)}([\omega]),\, [\sigma] \rangle.$$

Here $\varphi = (\varphi_M)_{M \in \mathrm{Ob}(M(k))}$ and we take the component at $H^n(X,Y)$. When $\varphi = \mathrm{comp}$, this gives exactly $\int_\sigma \omega$.

Alternative viewpoint. The comparison isomorphism is a generic point of $\mathcal{P}$.

Toy example. Take $k = \mathbb{Q}$, $X = \mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{Q}$, so $\mathcal{O}(X) = \mathbb{Q}[x]$. A point $\alpha \in X(\mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}$ gives:

$$\mathbb{Q}[x] \longrightarrow \mathbb{C}, \qquad P \longmapsto P(\alpha).$$

This is injective $\iff$ $\alpha$ is transcendental $\iff$ $\alpha$ is a generic point of $\mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{Q}$. The conjecture asserts that $\mathrm{comp}$ is a generic point of $\mathcal{P}$ (and in particular $\mathcal{P}$ should be irreducible).

Working Object by Object

$G_{\mathrm{mot}}(k)$ is too large to study directly. Given $M \in M(k)$, consider the smallest Tannakian subcategory $\langle M \rangle^{\otimes} \subset M(k)$ containing $M$. By Tannaka duality $\langle M \rangle^{\otimes} \simeq \mathrm{Rep}(G_M)$ where $G_M$ fits into:

Grothendieck Period Conjecture. For $X$ smooth and $Y \subset X$ s.n.c.:

$$\mathrm{tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\!\left(\text{periods of } H^n(X, Y)\right) = \dim_\mathbb{Q} G_{H^n(X,Y)}.$$

Putting everything together, this conjecture is equivalent to the Kontsevich–Zagier conjecture.

III. Cohomology Theories for Varieties with a Function

Motivation

We have said that there are numbers not expected to be periods:

$$e^\alpha \text{ for } \alpha \in \overline{\mathbb{Q}}^\times, \qquad \sqrt{\pi} = \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx, \qquad \gamma = \iint_{\substack{x \geq 0,\, y \leq 1}} e^{-xy}\,dx\,dy.$$

The current state of knowledge is very poor: we do not know 3 periods that are algebraically independent, the transcendence degree of the ring of periods could conceivably be as small as 2, and we do not know a single explicit non-period. The numbers above are only conjectured to not be periods.

Setup

Let $k \subset \mathbb{C}$, $X$ smooth over $k$, and $f \in \mathcal{O}_X(X)$ a regular function, i.e. a morphism $X \xrightarrow{f} \mathbb{A}^1_k$. If $f$ is non-constant, $X$ is not proper. We want to consider integrals $\int_\sigma e^{-f}\omega$, which should satisfy a Stokes formula. Indeed:

$$d(e^{-f}\omega) = e^{-f}(d\omega - df \wedge \omega).$$

Twisted de Rham Cohomology

Definition. The twisted de Rham cohomology of $(X, f)$ is:

$$H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X; f) := \mathbb{H}^n_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,\; \mathcal{O}_X \xrightarrow{d_f} \Omega^1_X \xrightarrow{d_f} \Omega^2_X \xrightarrow{d_f} \cdots\right),$$

where the twisted differential is $d_f(\omega) := d\omega - df \wedge \omega$.

This is indeed a complex: $d_f^2 = 0$ because $d_f = e^f \circ d \circ e^{-f}$, so $d_f^2 = e^f \circ d^2 \circ e^{-f} = 0$.

Remark. If $f$ is constant: $H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X; f) = H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$. Classical motives are a special case with $f = 0$.

Conceptual Explanation: Vector Bundles with Connection

Twisted de Rham cohomology is the de Rham cohomology of a vector bundle with integrable connection: a morphism

satisfying the Leibniz rule: $\nabla(g \cdot e) = e \otimes dg + g \cdot \nabla(e)$.

The trivial connection is $\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{O}_X$, $\nabla(1) = 0$, giving the usual de Rham complex $\mathcal{O}_X \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^1_X$.

In our case, $\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{O}_X$ with the twisted connection:

$$\nabla_f(1) = -df, \qquad \nabla_f(g) = dg - g\,df \quad \text{for } g \in \mathcal{O}_X.$$

The associated de Rham complex is exactly $(\Omega^\bullet_X, d_f)$.

Analytification and the Poincaré Lemma

On $X^{\mathrm{an}}$, consider the analytic horizontal sections $\ker(\nabla_f^{\mathrm{an}}) \subset \mathcal{O}_{X^{\mathrm{an}}}$.

Poincaré Lemma.

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR,an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}},\, f^{\mathrm{an}}) \simeq H^*(X^{\mathrm{an}},\, \ker(\nabla_f^{\mathrm{an}})).$$

In the trivial case: $\ker(\nabla^{\mathrm{an}}) = \underline{\mathbb{C}}_{X^{\mathrm{an}}}$ (constant sheaf). In the twisted case: a holomorphic $g$ satisfies $\nabla_f^{\mathrm{an}}(g) = 0 \iff dg = g\,df \iff g = c\cdot e^f$, so $\ker(\nabla_f^{\mathrm{an}}) = \mathbb{C}\cdot e^f \cong \underline{\mathbb{C}}_{X^{\mathrm{an}}}$.

Upshot. $H^*_{\mathrm{dR,an}}(X^{\mathrm{an}}, f^{\mathrm{an}}) \simeq H^*(X(\mathbb{C}), \mathbb{C})$ = usual singular cohomology of $X(\mathbb{C})$. This is a manifestation of the phenomenon of irregular singularities: algebraically the twisted cohomology can be very different from the classical one, but analytically it recovers the usual topology.

Example: $X = \mathbb{A}^1_k$, $f = x^n$, $n \geq 2$

Since $X$ is affine, hypercohomology reduces to the complex of global sections:

$$k[x] \xrightarrow{d_f} k[x]\,dx, \qquad P \longmapsto (P' - nx^{n-1}P)\,dx.$$

Degree 0. $H^0_{\mathrm{dR}}(X; f) = \ker(d_f) = 0$: the only solution of $P' = nx^{n-1}P$ is $P = c\cdot e^{x^n}$, which is not a polynomial.

Degree 1. $H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(X; f) = \mathrm{coker}(d_f)$. The classes $dx, x\,dx, \ldots, x^{n-2}\,dx$ are linearly independent in the cokernel. To see they span, use algebraic integration by parts:

$$d_f\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{n}\right) = -nx^{n-1}\,dx \implies x^{n-1}\,dx = 0,$$

$$d_f\!\left(-\tfrac{x}{n}\right) = x^n\,dx - \tfrac{1}{n}\,dx \implies x^n\,dx = \tfrac{1}{n}\,dx,$$

$$d_f\!\left(-\tfrac{x^2}{n}\right) = x^{n+1}\,dx - \tfrac{2}{n}\,x\,dx \implies x^{n+1}\,dx = \tfrac{2}{n}\,x\,dx,$$

and by induction every $x^j\,dx$ for $j \geq n-1$ is in the span of $\{dx, \ldots, x^{n-2}\,dx\}$. Therefore:

$$H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{A}^1_k;\, x^n) \cong k[dx] \oplus k[x\,dx] \oplus \cdots \oplus k[x^{n-2}\,dx], \quad \dim = n-1.$$

Exercise. Show that $H^1_{\mathrm{dR,an}}(\mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{C};\, x^n) = 0$ (any holomorphic 1-form is in the image of $d_f$). This confirms the Poincaré lemma, since $\mathbb{C}$ is contractible.

IV. Rapid Decay Homology

We want to integrate $e^{-f}\omega$ over chains, but convergence requires $e^{-f}$ to decay on the non-compact ends.

Setup

Define the right half-planes in $\mathbb{C}$:

$$S_r = \{z \in \mathbb{C} \mid \mathrm{Re}(z) > r\}, \qquad r \in \mathbb{R}.$$

On the preimage $f^{-1}(S_r(\mathbb{C})) \subset X(\mathbb{C})$ the function $e^{-f}$ decays exponentially.

Proposition. There exists a Zariski open $U \subset \mathbb{A}^1_k$ (the set of non-critical values, by Ehresmann's theorem) such that $f^{-1}(U(\mathbb{C})) \xrightarrow{f} U(\mathbb{C})$ is a locally trivial fibration: for every $x \in U(\mathbb{C})$, there exists an open $V_x \ni x$ such that $f^{-1}(x) \hookrightarrow f^{-1}(V_x)$ is a homotopy equivalence.

In particular, for $r$ large enough the transition maps in the system $(H^{\mathrm{sing}}_n(X(\mathbb{C}), f^{-1}(S_r)))_{r \to +\infty}$ are isomorphisms.

Definition (Rapid Decay Homology).

$$H^{\mathrm{Rd}}_n(X, f) := \varinjlim_{r \to +\infty} H^{\mathrm{sing}}_n\!\left(X(\mathbb{C}),\, f^{-1}(S_r);\, \mathbb{Q}\right), \qquad S_r = \{\mathrm{Re}(z) > r\}.$$

Summary. An element of $H^{\mathrm{Rd}}_n(X, f)$ is (for $r$ large enough) a chain $\sigma$ in $X(\mathbb{C})$ whose boundary lies in $f^{-1}(S_r) = \{\mathrm{Re}(f) > r\}$. Two chains are identified if they agree for all large $r$.

Special cases.

  • If $f$ is constant, the image lies in a single point, so $f^{-1}(S_r) = \emptyset$ for large $r$ and $H^{\mathrm{Rd}}_n(X, f) = H^B_n(X)$ (usual Betti homology).
  • The integral $\int_\sigma e^{-f}\omega$ converges for $\sigma \in H^{\mathrm{Rd}}_n(X, f)$, since $e^{-f}$ is exponentially small on $\{\mathrm{Re}(f) > r\}$.

Example: $X = \mathbb{A}^1_k$, $f = x^n$

The region $\{\mathrm{Re}(x^n) > r\}$ in $\mathbb{C}$ consists of $n$ Stokes sectors, separated by the anti-Stokes directions at angles $\frac{(2j+1)\pi}{n}$ for $j = 0, \ldots, n-1$. Each sector is a connected component where $|e^{-x^n}|$ decays as $|x| \to \infty$.

We have $H^{\mathrm{Rd}}_1(\mathbb{A}^1, x^n) \cong H^B_1(\mathbb{C}, \{n\text{ points}\})$, which has dimension $n-1$. A set of $n-1$ generators consists of paths connecting adjacent sectors at infinity (each path goes to $\infty$ inside those sectors, so has boundary in the rapid decay region). For $n = 2$: the single generator is the path along $\mathbb{R}$ from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$.

Integrating $e^{-x^n}\,dx$ along these cycles gives values proportional to $\Gamma(1/n)$ (via $t = x^n$):

$$\int_\sigma e^{-x^n}\,dx \sim \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right).$$

This gives the comparison isomorphism between $H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{A}^1; x^n)$ (of dimension $n-1$) and $H^{\mathrm{Rd}}_1(\mathbb{A}^1, x^n)$ (also of dimension $n-1$), with period matrix entries the values $\Gamma(j/n)$ for $j = 1, \ldots, n-1$.
