Recall from last time: for $k \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$ and $X$ a smooth algebraic variety over $k$, the comparison isomorphism gives

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \otimes_k \mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_B(X) \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{C}, \qquad [\omega] \longmapsto \left([\sigma] \mapsto \int_\sigma \omega\right).$$

The preliminary definition of a period was: any number of the form $\int_\sigma \omega$, whenever $k \subset \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.

Why preliminary? Because we would also like numbers such as

$$\log 2 = \int_1^2 \frac{dx}{x} = \int_\sigma \omega,$$

but here $\partial\sigma \neq 0$: the chain $\sigma = [1,2]$ has boundary $2 - 1 \neq 0$, so it is not a cycle.

Solution: use relative cohomology. We allow chains with non-trivial boundary, as long as the boundary is contained in a fixed subvariety.

I. Relative Cohomology

Let $Y \hookrightarrow X$ be a closed subvariety defined over $k$.

Betti Side

Definition. The relative Betti cohomology $H^*_B(X, Y)$ is the dual of the relative singular homology

$$H^B_*(X, Y) := H^{\mathrm{sing}}_*(X(\mathbb{C}), Y(\mathbb{C}),\, \mathbb{Q}),$$

which is the homology of the cone of the chain map $i_*: C_*(Y(\mathbb{C})) \to C_*(X(\mathbb{C}))$.

The map $i_*$ is injective (just composing a simplex in $Y$ with the inclusion), and the cone complex is:

$$\bigl(\mathrm{Cone}(i_*)\bigr)_n = C_{n-1}(Y(\mathbb{C})) \oplus C_n(X(\mathbb{C}))$$

with differential $\partial(a, b) = \bigl(-\partial a,\; \partial b + i_*(a)\bigr)$ for $a \in C_{n-1}(Y(\mathbb{C}))$, $b \in C_n(X(\mathbb{C}))$.

So concretely, an element of $H^B_n(X, Y)$ is represented by a pair $(\sigma_Y, \sigma_X)$ which is closed:

$$\partial \sigma_Y = 0, \qquad \partial \sigma_X + i_*(\sigma_Y) = 0.$$

Since $i_*$ is injective, this simplifies: you just need $\sigma_X$ with $\partial(\sigma_X) \in C_{n-1}(Y(\mathbb{C}))$.

This is exactly what we wanted: the boundary of $\sigma_X$ must lie in $Y(\mathbb{C})$.

By design, the construction gives a long exact sequence:

$$\cdots \to H^B_n(Y) \xrightarrow{i_*} H^B_n(X) \longrightarrow H^B_n(X,Y) \xrightarrow{\partial} H^B_{n-1}(Y) \to \cdots$$

So knowing the homology of $X$ and $Y$ at least controls the dimensions of the relative groups.

In terms of sheaves. Writing $Y \overset{i}{\hookrightarrow} X \overset{j}{\hookleftarrow} X\setminus Y$ (closed/open), one has

$$H^*_B(X, Y) \simeq H^*\!\left(X^{\mathrm{an}},\, j_! j^* \underline{\mathbb{Q}}_X\right).$$

There is a short exact sequence of sheaves

$$0 \longrightarrow j_! j^* \underline{\mathbb{Q}}_X \longrightarrow \underline{\mathbb{Q}}_X \longrightarrow i_* \underline{\mathbb{Q}}_Y \longrightarrow 0,$$

which gives exactly the long exact sequence above.

De Rham Side

Assume $Y$ is smooth. The closed immersion $i: Y \hookrightarrow X$ induces a morphism of de Rham complexes:

$$\Omega^\bullet_X \xrightarrow{i^*} i_* \Omega^\bullet_Y.$$

We now take the total complex $\mathrm{Tot}(i^*)$ of the double complex

$$\begin{array}{ccccc} \vdots & & \vdots \\ \Omega^2_X & \longrightarrow & i_*\Omega^2_Y \\ \uparrow & & \uparrow \\ \Omega^1_X & \longrightarrow & i_*\Omega^1_Y \\ \uparrow & & \uparrow \\ \mathcal{O}_X & \longrightarrow & i_*\mathcal{O}_Y \end{array}$$

and look at the diagonal. The total complex $\mathrm{Tot}(i^*)$ has differential

$$d(a,b) = \bigl(da,\; -db + i^*a\bigr), \qquad a \in \Omega^n_X,\; b \in i_*\Omega^{n-1}_Y.$$

Definition.

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y) := \mathbb{H}^*_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,\, \mathrm{Tot}(i^*)\right) \qquad \text{(hypercohomology)}.$$

Concretely (assuming $X$ affine, so global sections suffice): a class in $H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y)$ is represented by a pair

$$\omega_X \in \Omega^n_X(X) \quad \text{with} \quad d\omega_X = 0,$$

$$\eta_Y \in \Omega^{n-1}_Y(Y) \quad \text{with} \quad \omega_X\big|_Y = d\eta_Y.$$

The map $[(\omega_X, \eta_Y)] \mapsto [\omega_X]$ (forgetting $\eta_Y$) fits into the long exact sequence by design:

$$\cdots \longrightarrow H^{n-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(Y) \longrightarrow H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y) \longrightarrow H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(X) \longrightarrow H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(Y) \longrightarrow \cdots$$

Example: $X = \mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}$, $Y = \{1, 2\}$

Take $X = \mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}])$ (which is the right setting for $\frac{dx}{x}$) and $Y = \{1, 2\} = \{(x-1)(x-2) = 0\}$.

De Rham computation. The total complex of $i^*$ is:

$$\mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}] \longrightarrow \mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}]\,dx \;\oplus\; \mathbb{Q} \oplus \mathbb{Q}$$

$$P \longmapsto \bigl(P'(x)\,dx,\; P(1),\; P(2)\bigr)$$

(restriction to $Y$ is just evaluation; the rest of the complex is zero). Thus $H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y)$ is the cohomology of this map. We find the cokernel (things not in the image):

  • $\bigl(\frac{dx}{x}, 0, 0\bigr)$: certainly not in the image, since the logarithm is not algebraic.
  • $(0, 1, 0)$: not in the image either — if $P' = 0$ then $P$ is constant, so $P(1)$ must equal $P(2)$.

These two are linearly independent in the cokernel.

Exercise. Show that they form a basis.

Moreover, $(0, 1, 0)$ defines the same class as $\bigl(\frac{dx}{x}, 0, 0\bigr)$. Indeed, their difference

$$\left(\frac{dx}{x}, 0, 0\right) - (0, 1, 0) = \left(\frac{dx}{x}, -1, 0\right)$$

lies in the image: we need $P$ with $P' = 1$, $P(1) = -1$, $P(2) = 0$. Since $P' = 1 \Rightarrow P = x + c$, the system $1 + c = -1$, $2 + c = 0$ gives $c = -2$. So it is the image of $P(x) = x - 2$. Therefore:

$$H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}\!\left(\mathbb{A}^1_\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\},\; \{1,2\}\right) = \mathbb{Q}\bigl[dx\bigr] \oplus \mathbb{Q}\!\left[\tfrac{dx}{x}\right].$$

Betti computation. We have $X(\mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^*$ and $Y(\mathbb{C}) = \{1, 2\}$. The relevant piece of the long exact sequence is:

$$0 \to H^B_1(X) \to H^B_1(X, Y) \xrightarrow{\partial} H^B_0(Y) \to H^B_0(X) \to 0$$

$$\| \qquad\qquad\quad \| \qquad\qquad\qquad\quad \| \qquad\qquad\quad \|$$

$$\mathbb{Q}\cdot\gamma \qquad \bullet\!\to\!\bullet \qquad\quad \mathbb{Q} \oplus \mathbb{Q} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{sum}} \mathbb{Q}$$

The two generators of $H^B_1(X, Y)$ are: $\gamma$ (the loop $|x|=1$ around $0$) and the path from $1$ to $2$.

Period matrix. Integrating the two de Rham classes against the two Betti classes:

$$\begin{array}{c|cc} & dx & \dfrac{dx}{x} \\[6pt] \hline [1 \to 2] & 1 & \log 2 \\[6pt] \gamma & 0 & 2\pi i \end{array}$$

Theorem. There is a comparison isomorphism

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X, Y) \otimes_k \mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{\sim} H^*_B(X, Y) \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{C},$$

dual to the relative period pairing:

$$[(\omega_X, \eta_Y)] \otimes [\sigma_X] \longmapsto \int_{\sigma_X} \omega_X + \int_{\partial \sigma_X} \eta_Y.$$

Remark. This is still not enough in general: we need to extend to the case where $Y$ is not smooth, but merely has simple normal crossing (s.n.c.) singularities. (This is needed precisely because the compactification used in the proof of the comparison isomorphism only produces s.n.c. divisors.)

The (Final) Definition of a Period

Definition. A period is a coefficient of the comparison isomorphism for some $H^n(X, Y)$ over $k \subset \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, where $X$ is smooth and $Y$ is a simple normal crossing divisor.

Goal. Attach to any period a motivic Galois group. This requires understanding all algebraic relations between periods. That is: given periods $p_1, \ldots, p_r$, find all $Q \in \overline{\mathbb{Q}}[x_1, \ldots, x_r]$ with $Q(p_1, \ldots, p_r) = 0$.

Remark. It is enough to understand equality between periods. Indeed, periods form a $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$-algebra (closed under $+$ and $\cdot$, containing $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$), so any polynomial relation reduces to checking whether a given period equals zero.

Key point: Künneth formula. For both cohomologies (both de Rham and Betti, when not stated otherwise):

$$H^*(X_1, Y_1) \otimes H^*(X_2, Y_2) \simeq H^*\!\left(X_1 \times X_2,\; (Y_1 \times X_2) \cup (X_1 \times Y_2)\right).$$

Thus we just need to understand equality of periods. There are three sources of relations:

  1. Bilinearity of the period pairing: integrating over a sum of cycles is the sum of the integrals, both over cycles and over forms.

  2. Change of variables: for a morphism $f: X \to X'$,

    $$\int_\sigma f^*\omega = \int_{f_*\sigma} \omega.$$

  3. Stokes' formula:

    $$\int_\sigma d\omega = \int_{\partial\sigma} \omega.$$

    Note: in the relative context, $\partial\sigma$ is no longer necessarily zero.

Conjecture (Kontsevich–Zagier). Any relation between periods follows from these three.

In other words: if you have an equality between two periods, you can derive it by applying only these three types of relations. This will be made more formal later.

II. Nori Motives

Inspired by the Kontsevich–Zagier conjecture, Nori's construction gives one of the ways to define motives. The key feature is that we can define the motivic Galois group unconditionally; the conjecture then implies that it acts on periods (on all of them). Conjecturally, these groups are the Galois group of periods in a precise sense.

Quivers and Representations

Definition. A quiver is a directed graph — think of it as a category without composition (composition will be added later).

Definition. A representation of a quiver $Q$ is a functor $\rho: Q \to \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}$: to each vertex a vector space, and to each arrow a linear map.

The Endomorphism Algebra

Given a representation $\rho: Q \to \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}$, define:

$$\mathrm{End}(\rho) = \varprojlim_{\substack{Q_0 \subseteq Q \\ \text{finite}}} \mathrm{End}\!\left(\rho\big|_{Q_0}\right),$$

where the limit runs over all finite sub-quivers $Q_0$. This is a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra with a natural topology inherited as a limit of finite-dimensional algebras.

For a fixed finite $Q_0$, we define:

$$\mathrm{End}\!\left(\rho\big|_{Q_0}\right) = \left\{(e_q)_{q \in Q_0} \in \prod_{q \in Q_0} \mathrm{End}(\rho(q)) \;\middle|\; \text{compatible}\right\},$$

where compatible means: for every arrow $f: q \to q'$ in $Q_0$, the diagram

$$\begin{array}{ccc} \rho(q) & \xrightarrow{\rho(f)} & \rho(q') \\ e_q \downarrow & & \downarrow e_{q'} \\ \rho(q) & \xrightarrow{\rho(f)} & \rho(q') \end{array}$$

commutes. Therefore $\mathrm{End}(\rho)$ is a pro-finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra.

Definition. $\langle Q, \rho \rangle$ is the category of finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector spaces with a continuous action of $\mathrm{End}(\rho)$.

An object of $\langle Q, \rho \rangle$ is a vector space $V$ together with a ring map $\mathrm{End}(\rho) \to \mathrm{End}(V)$ that factors through $\mathrm{End}(\rho|_{Q_0})$ for some finite $Q_0$ — i.e., the action is continuous for the profinite topology. There are natural "functors" assembling into:

$$Q \xrightarrow{\rho} \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q} \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q \to \langle Q, \rho \rangle \xrightarrow{\text{forgetful}} \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}, \quad q \mapsto \rho(q).$$

Proposition (Nori) — Universal property. The construction $\langle Q, \rho \rangle$ is universal: whenever $\mathcal{A}$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear abelian category and $\beta: \mathcal{A} \to \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear exact faithful functor, any factorization of $\rho$ through $\mathcal{A}$ induces a unique (up to unique isomorphism) functor $\langle Q, \rho \rangle \to \mathcal{A}$:

$$\begin{array}{ccc} & & \mathcal{A} \\ & \nearrow & \downarrow \beta \\ Q & \xrightarrow{\rho} & \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q} \end{array}$$

The Nori Quiver $Q(k)$

We apply the above to the specific quiver $Q(k)$:

Vertices: symbols $[X, Y, n, i]$ where $X$ is a variety over $k$, $Y \subset X$ is closed, and $n, i \in \mathbb{Z}$ (with $n$ the cohomological degree and $i$ the Tate twist). Morally, the vertex $[X, Y, n, i]$ corresponds to $H^n(X, Y)$ with Tate twist $(i)$.

Arrows (three types, each inspired by one of the three sources of relations):

  • Type (a): For every morphism $h: X \to X'$ with $h(Y) \subset Y'$, a functoriality arrow:

    $$[X', Y', n, i] \xrightarrow{h^*} [X, Y, n, i].$$

  • Type (b): For every $Z \subset Y \subset X$ closed, a connecting homomorphism:

    $$[Y, Z, n-1, i] \xrightarrow{\partial} [X, Y, n, i].$$

  • Type (c): For every $Y \subset X$ closed, a Tate/Künneth arrow:

    $$\left[X \times \mathbb{G}_m,\; (Y \times \mathbb{G}_m) \cup (X \times \{1\}),\; n+1,\; i+1\right] \longrightarrow [X, Y, n, i].$$

The Quiver Representation $\rho_B$

The Betti representation is:

$$\rho_B: Q(k) \longrightarrow \mathrm{Vect}_\mathbb{Q}, \qquad [X, Y, n, i] \longmapsto H^n_B(X, Y)(i),$$

where the Tate twist $(i)$ means tensoring $(-i)$ times with

$$H^1_B(\mathbb{G}_m) \simeq H^1_B(\mathbb{G}_m, \{1\}) = \mathbb{Q}(-1).$$

Concretely: for $i > 0$, tensor $i$ times; for $i < 0$, tensor $(-i)$ times with the dual.

The arrows are mapped as follows:

  • (a): $H^n_B(X', Y')(i) \xrightarrow{h^*} H^n_B(X, Y)(i)$.
  • (b): $H^{n-1}_B(Y, Z)(i) \xrightarrow{\partial} H^n_B(X, Y)(i)$.
  • (c): By the Künneth formula: $$H^{n+1}_B\!\bigl(X \times \mathbb{G}_m,\; (Y \times \mathbb{G}_m) \cup (X \times \{1\})\bigr)(i+1) \;=\; \bigoplus_{a+b=n+1} H^a_B(X,Y) \otimes H^b_B(\mathbb{G}_m, 1) \cdot (i+1).$$ Since $H^b_B(\mathbb{G}_m, 1) = 0$ for $b \neq 1$ and $= H^1_B(\mathbb{G}_m)$ for $b = 1$ (because $\mathbb{G}_m = \mathbb{C}^*$ is connected, so $H^0 \simeq \mathbb{Q}$, and taking the relative group with respect to $\{1\}$ kills it), this collapses to $\simeq H^n_B(X, Y)(i)$, giving the required isomorphism.

The Category of Nori Motives

To have a category with duals, one formally introduces the dual via the Tate twist (the sign convention $-i$ in the twist). Then:

Definition (Nori Motives). The category of Nori motives over $k$ is

$$M(k) = \langle Q(k),\; \rho_B \rangle.$$

Remark. $M(k)$ is an abelian category. A Nori motive "is" a $H^n_B(X, Y)(i)$, or whatever one can obtain from these by taking kernels, cokernels, and extensions.

We will see informally that $M(k)$ has a tensor product $\otimes$, making it equivalent to the category of representations of a pro-algebraic group — the motivic Galois group.

Relation with Voevodsky Motives

There exists a functor from the derived category $D^b(M(k))$ to Voevodsky's triangulated category of motives $DM(k)$, and conjecturally this functor is an equivalence. So Voevodsky motives should be the derived category of Nori motives.
